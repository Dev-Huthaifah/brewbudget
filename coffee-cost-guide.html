A PRACTICAL COFFEE BUDGET GUIDE

How much does your coffee cost at home?

A useful comparison starts with what you actually pay for beans and the way you brew. Here’s a simple method you can adjust to your cup, equipment, and currency.

Quick formula

Cost per cup = beans + filters + water + energy + extras

For beans, divide the bag price by the number of cups you can make from that bag. Add equipment separately if you want to include it.

1. Work out the bean cost for one cup

Check the weight and price of your coffee bag, then decide how many grams you use for one drink. Divide the bag weight by your dose to estimate how many cups it makes. Divide the bag price by that cup count.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE

A 250 g bag costs 18 currency units. If you use 18 g per cup, the bag makes about 13.9 cups. The beans cost about 1.30 units per cup (18 ÷ 13.9). This example is only arithmetic; your bag price and recipe may be different.

A scale makes the estimate more consistent, but you can also use your usual scoop and measure the bag’s servings over a few brews. Espresso, filter, and batch coffee use different amounts, so use the amount that matches your routine.

2. Add the costs you want to count

Beans are the main recurring cost for many people, but they are not the whole cup. Add any costs that matter to your comparison:

  • Filters: divide the price of a pack by the number of filters you use.
  • Milk, syrups, or other additions: estimate the amount used in one drink.
  • Water and energy: include them if you track household costs closely.
  • Waste: account for beans or milk that are regularly discarded.

Keep the estimate consistent. If your café price includes milk but your home estimate does not, add the home ingredients too for a fairer comparison.

3. Decide how to treat equipment

A grinder, kettle, brewer, or espresso machine is usually an upfront purchase. You can leave equipment out when comparing only recurring costs, or spread its price across the cups you expect to make before replacing it.

Equipment cost per cup = equipment price ÷ expected cups made

For example, if a brewer costs 60 units and you expect to make 600 cups with it, that is 0.10 units per cup. The result depends on your own estimate of how long the equipment will last.

4. Compare like with like

Use the same number of drinks and the same time period for both options. A simple weekly café estimate is:

Weekly café spendcups per day × café price × days per weekYearly home-brew costhome cost per cup × cups per day × days per week × 52

The comparison is an estimate, not a promise that home brewing will save money. Equipment, changing prices, travel, taxes, and changes in your routine can change the result.

Common questions

Should I include my coffee machine?

That depends on the comparison you want. Leave it out to compare recurring cup costs, or divide its price by your estimated lifetime number of cups to include an equipment cost.

What should count as one cup?

Use one drink as you normally make or buy it. A café drink and a home serving can differ in size, so note that if it affects your comparison.

Does BrewBudget use a live exchange rate?

No. The currency selector only changes the currency label and formatting. Enter all café and home prices in the same selected currency.
